How to Share Audio and Music Files Between Devices

A practical guide from PairDrop.

Audio files are smaller than video, but they have their own problems: formats that one device cannot play, voice notes that arrive as a link instead of a file, and music that loses quality when it is compressed again. Whether you are sharing a song, a podcast episode, a voice memo or a recording of a meeting, you want the recipient to hear it clearly and be able to play it. This guide explains audio formats in plain language, which methods keep quality, and how to fix the common problems that make an audio file silent or unplayable.

Know the common audio formats

Most audio you share will be MP3, WAV, AAC, FLAC or a voice memo format. MP3 is small and plays almost everywhere, but it is compressed. WAV is large and uncompressed, and is best for editing. AAC is common on Apple devices and plays widely. FLAC keeps full quality in a smaller file than WAV, but not every player supports it. Voice memos may use a format that only works in the app that recorded them. If you are unsure, MP3 is the safest choice for sharing, because almost every phone and computer can play it. For editing or archiving, keep the original format and share a copy in MP3 if needed.

Share audio as a file to keep quality

Send an audio file as a file, not as a message that may compress it. A cable, a local Wi-Fi transfer, a USB drive or a cloud link to the original file all keep the sound unchanged. Check the file size after the transfer: a much smaller file was probably recompressed. For a voice note, use the share option in the recording app and choose to send the file, not a link, where possible. For music, keep the file name with the artist and title, so the recipient can find it later. A clear name is as important as good sound.

Fix audio that will not play

If an audio file will not play, the problem is usually the format or the player. Try opening it in a different app, such as a music player or a media player that supports more formats. Check that the file downloaded fully and is not zero bytes, which means the transfer failed. If the file plays on one device but not another, convert a copy to MP3 and send that copy. For a recording that sounds quiet or distorted, the problem may be the original recording, not the transfer, so listen to the original on the sending device before you blame the sharing method.

Organise music and recordings as you share them

Audio files multiply quickly: songs, voice notes, podcasts and meeting recordings can fill a phone. Create folders by type, such as Music, Voice Notes and Recordings, and name files with a date and a topic. Delete recordings you no longer need after you have shared them, and back up important recordings to a computer or a drive. A tidy audio library makes it easy to find a recording months later, and it stops your phone from filling up with files you have forgotten.

Quick tips

  • Send audio as a file, not a compressed message
  • MP3 plays almost everywhere
  • Check the file size after transfer
  • Name files with artist, title and date

Frequently asked questions

What audio format should I share?

MP3 is the safest choice because almost every device can play it. For editing or archiving, keep the original WAV or FLAC file as well.

Why is my voice note quiet after sharing?

It may have been compressed, or the recording itself was quiet. Check the original, send the file directly, and avoid methods that recompress audio.

Why will an audio file not play on the other device?

The player may not support the format. Try a different media app, or send a copy converted to MP3.
